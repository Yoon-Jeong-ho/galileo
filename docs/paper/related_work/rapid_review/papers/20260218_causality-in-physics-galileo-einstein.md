# Causality in Physics: From Galileo to Einstein, and Beyond

- Venue: arXiv (physics.pop-ph)

## One-sentence takeaway

A high-level historical/conceptual tour arguing that “causality” is central to intuition and practice but often not explicitly encoded in the formalism of successful physical theories.

## What problem does it solve?

- Clarifies (for a broad physics/philosophy audience) how different formalisms (Newtonian, Lagrangian/Hamiltonian, fields, relativity, quantum/stat mech) treat causal language vs. what the equations actually specify.
- Motivates why “cause → effect” is subtle when fundamental laws are time-reversal symmetric and when causality is enforced via boundary conditions / locality constraints rather than an explicit primitive.

## What is the core method / protocol?

- Expository essay / talk-style narrative.
- Uses canonical examples and conceptual pivots (inertia/force, inertial forces and frames, least action, fields/retarded solutions, Russell’s critique, light cones in relativity) to illustrate how “causality” changes role across frameworks.

## What are the key metrics?

- None (no experiments / benchmarks; not a technical ML/CS paper).

## What are the main results?

- Argues there is no single, general formal definition of causality used across physics, despite heavy causal talk in explanation.
- Highlights that:
  - Newton’s laws can be read both ways (forces → acceleration vs. forces as bookkeeping for observed acceleration, especially in non-inertial frames).
  - Variational principles can look “teleological” though they’re equivalent to local differential equations.
  - In field theories, causal direction often comes from selecting physically reasonable (retarded) solutions, not from the equations alone.
  - In relativity, causal structure becomes explicit via light cones / finite signal speed, yet still framed in geometric constraints more than “cause” primitives.
