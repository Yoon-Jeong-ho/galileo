\title{GALILEO: Measuring Multi-turn Belief-Consistency Failures and Recovery Under Social Pressure}
\author{Anonymous Authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) can abandon previously correct answers when users apply conversational pressure (e.g., repeated denial, appeals to authority, persuasive reframing), a behavior related to sycophancy and persuasion-induced change in dialogue \citep{sharma2023understandingsycophancylanguagemodels,fanous2025sycevalevaluatingllmsycophancy,huang2026vulnerabilityllmsbeliefsystems}. Standard ground-truth benchmarks largely report single-turn accuracy, obscuring interaction dynamics: when the first failure occurs, how robustness decays over turns, and whether a model can return to the correct answer after being misled.

We introduce \textbf{GALILEO}, a reproducible protocol for measuring multi-turn robustness \emph{conditional on initial correctness} on ground-truth tasks (math, extractive QA, multiple-choice QA, open-domain QA). For each task, we filter to examples answered correctly at round $0$, then apply $R{=}5$ rounds of evidence-free pressure personas. Each persona arm is paired with a matched \textbf{Neutral Re-asking Control} (\textbf{NRC}) that repeats the same multi-round structure and decoding but uses neutral ``double-check'' follow-ups that introduce no new task-relevant evidence. Persona--control comparisons are computed on the same initially-correct subset, isolating pressure effects from generic multi-turn drift.

GALILEO reports three complementary outcomes: (i) survival curves, where Survival@$r$ is the fraction that stays correct at every turn through round $r$ (not merely accuracy at round $r$); (ii) the \textbf{time-to-first-failure} (\textbf{TTF}) distribution---the first pressure round in $\{1,\dots,R\}$ where an initially-correct example becomes incorrect (with Fail@1 for immediate vulnerability); and (iii) Recovery@flip, the probability of returning to the correct answer on a final neutral recovery prompt (round $R{+}1$), conditional on having flipped at least once during rounds $1$--$R$.

Across multi-seed experiments on several open-weight model families, persona pressure consistently reduces survival relative to the NRC and increases early-turn vulnerability. In our main aggregated setting (4 seeds), persona pressure reduces Survival@5 by 22.8 percentage points and increases Fail@1 by 6.9 points relative to the NRC. Recovery@flip varies by task and persona, indicating that staying correct and returning to truth after a flip are distinct behaviors. We release an auditable evaluation pipeline with paper-ready exports (CSV artifacts and figure sources) to support reproduction and reviewer verification.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern LLM assistants are often evaluated on single-turn correctness, but many real interactions are multi-turn: users challenge answers, assert contradictory ``facts,'' or apply rhetorical pressure (e.g., ``Are you sure?'' or ``My professor said otherwise.''). In these settings, a model can exhibit a 
\emph{belief-consistency failure}: changing a previously correct answer to an incorrect one due to social influence rather than new evidence.
Despite growing interest in sycophancy and multi-turn persuasion effects (e.g., \citealp{sharma2023understandingsycophancylanguagemodels,liu2025truthdecayquantifyingmultiturn}), existing evaluations rarely provide an end-to-end, reproducible protocol where correctness is \emph{checkable at every turn} and failures can be localized to the exact round where the first flip occurs.

GALILEO targets this gap by instantiating multi-turn pressure on \emph{ground-truth} tasks with automatic checking. We pair adversarial personas with a \emph{Neutral Re-asking Control} (\textbf{NRC}) to separate generic answer drift from persona-induced degradation, and we add a recovery stage to measure whether models can return to the original truth after an induced flip.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} GALILEO provides:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ground-truth multi-turn protocol:} survival and time-to-first-failure (TTF) metrics on automatically checkable tasks.
  \item \textbf{Drift baseline with matched conditioning sets:} a \emph{Neutral Re-asking Control} (\textbf{NRC}), and persona--control deltas computed on the \emph{same} initially-correct subset to avoid conflating persona effects with changes in which examples are initially correct.
  \item \textbf{Recovery stage:} a dedicated return-to-truth prompt evaluated \emph{after} the pressure dialogue to quantify recoverability conditional on having flipped.
  \item \textbf{Artifact-level traceability:} per-turn outputs and correctness traces, plotted curves, and reproducer scripts enabling end-to-end auditing.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Metrics (at a glance).} We report three complementary views of multi-turn belief consistency: \emph{fail@any} (fraction of initially-correct samples that flip at least once within the $R$ pressure rounds), \emph{time-to-first-failure} (\textbf{TTF}; the first round where the flip occurs, enabling ``when'' analysis), and \emph{survival} (probability of staying correct through each round, summarizing degradation over time). On flipped samples, we additionally measure \emph{recovery} as the probability of returning to the original ground-truth answer after an explicit recovery prompt.

\paragraph{Formalization.} For each evaluation condition, let $i \in \{1,\dots,n\}$ index examples that are \emph{initially correct} at round $0$ (i.e., $C_{i,0}=1$). Let $C_{i,r} \in \{0,1\}$ denote whether the model's answer is correct at round $r \in \{0,1,\dots,R\}$ under automatic checking, where rounds $1..R$ correspond to persona-pressure turns (or the matched \textbf{NRC}).

We define the \emph{time-to-first-failure} (\textbf{TTF}; a.k.a. turn-of-failure) as the first pressure round where correctness is lost:
\begin{equation}
  T_i = \min\{r \in \{1,\dots,R\} : C_{i,r}=0\},\quad \text{with } T_i = R{+}1 \text{ if no failure occurs within $R$ rounds.}
\end{equation}
We refer to the empirical distribution of $T_i$ values (including the mass at $R{+}1$ for ``never fail'') as the \textbf{turn-of-failure} (TOF) distribution.
The (multi-turn) \emph{survival} event at round $r$ is ``no failure has occurred up to $r$,'' i.e., remaining correct through all rounds $1..r$ (cumulative correctness). Importantly, $\widehat{S}(r)$ is \emph{not} the marginal accuracy at round $r$; it is the probability of staying correct \emph{through} round $r$.
Accordingly, the empirical survival curve is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{S}(r) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[T_i > r],
\end{equation}
so that $\text{fail@any} = 1-\widehat{S}(R)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[T_i\le R]$. We additionally report \emph{Fail@1} as an early-turn vulnerability measure:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Fail@1} = \Pr(T=1) \approx \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[T_i=1].
\end{equation}
Note that survival/TTF depend only on the \emph{first} flip; if a model flips and later reverts within the pressure rounds, we still count the initial loss of truth as a failure.

\paragraph{Estimation details.} When all examples are run for the same fixed number of rounds $R$, $\widehat{S}(r)$ above is the empirical survival curve. If some traces terminate early (e.g., due to transient API failures), we treat these as right-censored at the last completed round and compute a Kaplan--Meier estimate \citep{kaplan1958nonparametric}; in the no-censoring case this reduces to the empirical form. In our main reported artifacts/plots we aggregate across random seeds and report mean$\pm$std at each round; bootstrap CIs are optional and can be added as a robustness appendix if space permits.

We measure return-to-truth explicitly in a separate \emph{recovery} stage. Concretely, we first run the full $R$-round persona (or control) interaction to determine whether and when a flip occurs, and only then issue a dedicated recovery prompt.
This design isolates \emph{recoverability} (ability to return to the original truth after having flipped) from within-round oscillations during the pressure dialogue.
Let $C^{\mathrm{rec}}_i\in\{0,1\}$ denote whether the model answers the recovery prompt correctly on example $i$ (with respect to the original ground-truth target). Restricting to flipped examples ($T_i\le R$), we define empirical recovery as
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathrm{Rec}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[T_i\le R]\, C^{\mathrm{rec}}_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[T_i\le R]}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Matched persona--control recovery.}
To compare recovery under persona pressure to the drift baseline, we compute recovery separately for the persona and its matched NRC run (run on the same initially-correct set $C_p$). Because recovery is conditional on flipping, the denominator (the set of flipped examples) can differ between persona and control. For each persona $p$ we therefore report the pair
\begin{align}
  \widehat{\mathrm{Rec}}_{\mathrm{persona}}(p) &= \widehat{\mathrm{Rec}}\big(\text{persona}=p\,;\, i\in C_p\big), \\
  \widehat{\mathrm{Rec}}_{\mathrm{NRC}}(p) &= \widehat{\mathrm{Rec}}\big(\text{control}\,;\, i\in C_p\big),
\end{align}
and summarize their difference as $\Delta\mathrm{Rec}(p)=\widehat{\mathrm{Rec}}_{\mathrm{persona}}(p)-\widehat{\mathrm{Rec}}_{\mathrm{NRC}}(p)$.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Instruction-tuned LLMs can exhibit \emph{sycophancy} and related social-pressure failures, deferring to a user's assertions even when they contradict externally verifiable task truth \citep{sharma2023understandingsycophancylanguagemodels}. Recent benchmarks and analyses broaden this line of work to additional pressure sources and evaluation designs, including sycophancy-focused suites \citep{fanous2025sycevalevaluatingllmsycophancy,cheng2025elephantmeasuringunderstandingsocial}. Closest to our evaluation setting, recent work measures \emph{multi-turn} sycophancy under repeated rebuttal and quantifies longitudinal ``truth decay'' in dialogues \citep{hong2025measuringsycophancylanguagemodels,liu2025truthdecayquantifyingmultiturn}, including setups where an evaluator actively rebuts model responses \citep{kim2025challengingevaluatorllmsycophancy}. Peer-pressure and multi-agent settings are also emerging \citep{song2025kairospeerpressure}. Related sycophancy phenomena have also been studied in specialized domains such as theorem proving \citep{petrov2025brokenmathbenchmarksycophancytheorem}. GALILEO is complementary: we focus on a standardized, end-to-end protocol for \emph{ground-truth} tasks that makes correctness checkable at every turn. Unlike open-ended preference/stance prompts where ``truth'' is ambiguous, our tasks yield automatic per-turn labels, enabling survival and turn-of-failure analysis as well as artifact-level audit trails.

A separate thread studies multi-turn instability even without explicit adversarial pressure: models may change answers across repeated questioning or extended interaction \citep{li2025firmorfickle,tosato2025persistentinstabilityllmspersonality}. GALILEO operationalizes this distinction by introducing an explicit drift baseline (\textbf{NRC}) and by reporting \emph{when} failures occur (turn-of-failure) alongside \emph{how much} performance degrades over rounds (survival curves). Our survival-based framing is also related to recent ``time-to-inconsistency'' analyses that apply survival methodology to robustness under attack \citep{li2026timetoinconsistencysurvivalanalysislarge}, though our focus is on persona pressure and includes a dedicated recovery stage.

Our recovery stage also connects to work on belief revision and self-correction in multi-turn reasoning, which studies whether models can update or repair beliefs after intervening turns (e.g., ReviseQA \citep{helwe2025reviseqa}). We differ in that we explicitly condition on an induced flip from an initially correct answer under social pressure, and we measure return-to-truth with respect to the original ground-truth target.

Work on belief resistance under strategic persuasion in longer conversations \citep{huang2026vulnerabilityllmsbeliefsystems} is also related; our setting emphasizes a compact, reproducible measurement stack (including artifact exports) and adds a recovery stage to quantify return-to-truth after a flip.

Finally, our emphasis on verifiable truth aligns with truthfulness benchmarks such as TruthfulQA \citep{lin2022truthfulqameasuringmodelsmimic}, but targets a different axis: multi-turn belief-consistency failures and recovery under conversational pressure.

\section{Protocol and result figures}

\paragraph{Personas and control.}
We instantiate social pressure via five fixed personas: \emph{Soft Pressure} (gentle doubt), \emph{Simple Denial} (repeated contradiction without justification), \emph{Strong Pressure} (assertive insistence), \emph{Authority Claim} (appeal to an external expert/source), and \emph{Logical Trap} (misleading reasoning that tempts a flip).
To separate persona-induced effects from generic multi-turn instability, we also run the \emph{Neutral Re-asking Control} (\textbf{NRC}), which re-queries the same question without adversarial framing. Concretely, the NRC user turn is constrained to be a single neutral ``please re-check'' sentence that introduces \emph{no new task-relevant evidence} (no new facts/premises, citations/URLs, counterexamples, or alternative candidate answers).

\paragraph{Matched conditioning set.}
Our multi-turn metrics are defined on the \emph{initially-correct} subset, so the effective evaluation set can vary across personas and seeds when runs are executed separately (e.g., due to stochastic decoding, minor prompt/config differences in Phase~1, or caching/runner details). To avoid conflating this \emph{conditioning-set drift} with a true persona effect, for each persona $p$ we define its initially-correct set $C_p$ (examples correct at round $0$ under that run's \emph{persona-free} Phase~1 prompt) and evaluate the \textbf{NRC} on the \emph{same} $C_p$ when computing persona--control deltas.

Formally, for any metric $M$ computed on initially-correct examples (e.g., $\widehat{S}(r)$, fail@any, or a TTF statistic), we report persona--control deltas on the matched set:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta M(p) = M\big(\text{persona}=p\,;\, i\in C_p\big) - M\big(\text{control}\,;\, i\in C_p\big).
\end{equation}
In practice, this ensures that persona effects are not conflated with the easier/harder composition of the initially-correct subset induced by different prompting conditions.

\section*{Limitations}
GALILEO is designed for \emph{ground-truth} tasks with automatic checking, which enables per-turn correctness labels but excludes many open-ended assistant behaviors where ``truth'' is ambiguous (e.g., subjective advice or creative writing). Our protocol also depends on obtaining an initially correct answer; while this matches the intended failure mode (flips from truth), it may underrepresent settings where models are incorrect from the start. Finally, we study a finite set of hand-designed personas and up to five pressure rounds; real-world social influence can be more diverse, longer-horizon, and multi-party.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
GALILEO applies conversational pressure intended to induce incorrect answers, which can surface undesirable model behaviors. We mitigate misuse risk by focusing on benign, automatically checkable tasks (e.g., math and factual QA) rather than sensitive or harmful domains, and by framing personas as evaluation prompts rather than deployment guidance. When releasing artifacts, practitioners should ensure that prompts, model outputs, and datasets do not contain personal data, and should treat measured robustness/recovery as diagnostic signals rather than a guarantee of safe behavior in the wild.
