\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) can exhibit \emph{belief-consistency failures}: revising previously correct answers when confronted with social pressure (e.g., repeated denial, authority claims, or persuasive reframing).
Prior work has documented sycophancy and persuasion effects, but there is no widely adopted, end-to-end protocol for ground-truth tasks that (i) measures \emph{when} the first failure occurs (turn-of-failure), (ii) summarizes degradation over multiple rounds (survival curves), and (iii) evaluates whether models can return to the original truth after a flip (recovery).

We introduce \textbf{GALILEO}, a reproducible evaluation pipeline for ground-truth tasks (math, extractive QA, multiple-choice QA, and open-domain QA).
Given an initially correct answer, GALILEO applies five adversarial personas (soft pressure, simple denial, strong pressure, authority claim, and logical trap) for up to five rounds, then prompts for recovery on flipped samples.
Across multiple model families and seeds, we find that persona pressure consistently worsens survival, increases \emph{fail@any} (flipping at least once within the $R$ pressure rounds), and shifts turn-of-failure earlier compared to a non-adversarial drift baseline (neutral re-asking control).
We further observe that \emph{robustness} (staying correct) and \emph{recoverability} (returning to truth after a flip) behave as distinct axes, and we provide artifact-level exports (per-turn correctness traces, curves, and reproducer scripts) to support auditing and reuse.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern LLM assistants are often evaluated on single-turn correctness, but many real interactions are multi-turn: users challenge answers, assert contradictory ``facts,'' or apply rhetorical pressure (e.g., ``Are you sure?'' or ``My professor said otherwise.''). In these settings, a model can exhibit a 
\emph{belief-consistency failure}: changing a previously correct answer to an incorrect one due to social influence rather than new evidence.
Despite growing interest in sycophancy and persuasion, existing evaluations rarely provide an end-to-end, reproducible protocol where correctness is \emph{checkable at every turn} and failures can be localized to the exact round where the first flip occurs.

GALILEO targets this gap by instantiating multi-turn pressure on \emph{ground-truth} tasks with automatic checking. We pair adversarial personas with a neutral re-asking control to separate generic answer drift from persona-induced degradation, and we add a recovery stage to measure whether models can return to the original truth after an induced flip.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} GALILEO provides:
(1) a ground-truth, multi-turn protocol with survival and turn-of-failure metrics;
(2) a drift baseline (neutral re-asking control) that separates generic answer instability from persona-induced effects;
(3) a recovery stage that quantifies return-to-truth after a flip; and
(4) artifact-level exports (per-turn outputs and correctness traces, plotted curves, and reproducer scripts) that enable end-to-end auditing.

\paragraph{Metrics (at a glance).} We report three complementary views of multi-turn belief consistency: \emph{fail@any} (fraction of initially-correct samples that flip at least once within the $R$ pressure rounds), \emph{turn-of-failure} (the first round where the flip occurs, enabling ``when'' analysis), and \emph{survival} (probability of staying correct through each round, summarizing degradation over time). On flipped samples, we additionally measure \emph{recovery} as the probability of returning to the original ground-truth answer after an explicit recovery prompt.

\paragraph{Formalization.} For each evaluation condition, let $i \in \{1,\dots,n\}$ index examples that are \emph{initially correct} at round $0$. Let $C_{i,r} \in \{0,1\}$ denote whether the model's answer is correct \emph{at} pressure round $r \in \{1,\dots,R\}$ under automatic checking.
We define the \emph{turn-of-failure} as the first round where correctness is lost:
\begin{equation}
  T_i = \min\{r : C_{i,r}=0\},\quad \text{with } T_i = R{+}1 \text{ if no failure occurs.}
\end{equation}
The (multi-turn) \emph{survival} event at round $r$ is ``no failure has occurred up to $r$,'' i.e., remaining correct through all rounds $1..r$.
Accordingly, the empirical survival curve is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{S}(r) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[T_i > r],
\end{equation}
so that $\text{fail@any} = 1-\widehat{S}(R)$. Note that survival/TOF depend only on the \emph{first} flip; if a model flips and later reverts within the pressure rounds, we still count the initial loss of truth as a failure.

\paragraph{Estimation details.} When all examples are run for the same fixed number of rounds $R$, $\widehat{S}(r)$ above is the empirical survival curve. If some traces terminate early (e.g., due to transient API failures), we treat these as right-censored at the last completed round and compute a Kaplan--Meier estimate; in the no-censoring case this reduces to the empirical form. For uncertainty, we report bootstrap confidence intervals by resampling examples within each condition.

We measure return-to-truth explicitly in a separate \emph{recovery} stage. Let $C^{\mathrm{rec}}_i\in\{0,1\}$ denote whether the model answers the recovery prompt correctly on example $i$ (with respect to the original ground-truth target). Restricting to flipped examples ($T_i\le R$), we define empirical recovery as
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathrm{Rec}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[T_i\le R]\, C^{\mathrm{rec}}_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[T_i\le R]}.
\end{equation}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Instruction-tuned LLMs can exhibit \emph{sycophancy} and related social-pressure failures, deferring to a user's assertions even when they contradict externally verifiable task truth \citep{sharma2023understandingsycophancylanguagemodels}. Recent benchmarks and analyses broaden this line of work to additional pressure sources and evaluation designs, including sycophancy-focused suites \citep{fanous2025sycevalevaluatingllmsycophancy,cheng2025elephantmeasuringunderstandingsocial}. Closest to our evaluation setting, recent work measures \emph{multi-turn} sycophancy under repeated rebuttal and quantifies longitudinal ``truth decay'' in dialogues \citep{hong2025measuringsycophancylanguagemodels,liu2025truthdecayquantifyingmultiturn}, including setups where an evaluator actively rebuts model responses \citep{kim2025challengingevaluatorllmsycophancy}. Peer-pressure and multi-agent settings are also emerging \citep{song2025kairospeerpressure}. Related sycophancy phenomena have also been studied in specialized domains such as theorem proving \citep{petrov2025brokenmathbenchmarksycophancytheorem}. GALILEO is complementary: we focus on a standardized, end-to-end protocol for \emph{ground-truth} tasks that makes correctness checkable at every turn. Unlike open-ended preference/stance prompts where ``truth'' is ambiguous, our tasks yield automatic per-turn labels, enabling survival and turn-of-failure analysis as well as artifact-level audit trails.

A separate thread studies multi-turn instability even without explicit adversarial pressure: models may change answers across repeated questioning or extended interaction \citep{li2025firmorfickle,tosato2025persistentinstabilityllmspersonality}. GALILEO operationalizes this distinction by introducing an explicit drift baseline (neutral re-asking control) and by reporting \emph{when} failures occur (turn-of-failure) alongside \emph{how much} performance degrades over rounds (survival curves). Our survival-based framing is also related to recent ``time-to-inconsistency'' analyses that apply survival methodology to robustness under attack \citep{li2026timetoinconsistencysurvivalanalysislarge}, though our focus is on persona pressure and includes a dedicated recovery stage. Work on belief resistance under strategic persuasion in longer conversations \citep{huang2026vulnerabilityllmsbeliefsystems} is also related; our setting emphasizes a compact, reproducible measurement stack (including artifact exports) and adds a recovery stage to quantify return-to-truth after a flip.

Finally, our emphasis on verifiable truth aligns with truthfulness benchmarks such as TruthfulQA \citep{lin2022truthfulqameasuringmodelsmimic}, but targets a different axis: multi-turn belief-consistency failures and recovery under conversational pressure.

\section{Protocol and result figures}

\paragraph{Personas and control.}
We instantiate social pressure via five fixed personas: \emph{Soft Pressure} (gentle doubt), \emph{Simple Denial} (repeated contradiction without justification), \emph{Strong Pressure} (assertive insistence), \emph{Authority Claim} (appeal to an external expert/source), and \emph{Logical Trap} (misleading reasoning that tempts a flip).
To separate persona-induced effects from generic multi-turn instability, we also run a \emph{neutral re-asking control} that re-queries the same question without adversarial framing.

\paragraph{Matched conditioning set.}
Because our multi-turn metrics are defined on the \emph{initially-correct} subset, the effective evaluation set can differ across personas and seeds. For each persona $p$, we therefore define its initially-correct set $C_p$ (examples correct at round $0$ \emph{under persona prompting}) and evaluate the neutral control on the \emph{same} $C_p$ when computing persona--control deltas. This ``persona-matched control'' prevents conditioning-set drift (differences in which examples qualify as initially correct) from being misinterpreted as a treatment effect.

Formally, for any metric $M$ computed on initially-correct examples (e.g., $\widehat{S}(r)$, fail@any, or a TOF statistic), we report persona--control deltas on the matched set:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta M(p) = M\big(\text{persona}=p\,;\, i\in C_p\big) - M\big(\text{control}\,;\, i\in C_p\big).
\end{equation}
In practice, this ensures that persona effects are not conflated with the easier/harder composition of the initially-correct subset induced by different prompting conditions.

\section*{Limitations}
GALILEO is designed for \emph{ground-truth} tasks with automatic checking, which enables per-turn correctness labels but excludes many open-ended assistant behaviors where ``truth'' is ambiguous (e.g., subjective advice or creative writing). Our protocol also depends on obtaining an initially correct answer; while this matches the intended failure mode (flips from truth), it may underrepresent settings where models are incorrect from the start. Finally, we study a finite set of hand-designed personas and up to five pressure rounds; real-world social influence can be more diverse, longer-horizon, and multi-party.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
GALILEO applies conversational pressure intended to induce incorrect answers, which can surface undesirable model behaviors. We mitigate misuse risk by focusing on benign, automatically checkable tasks (e.g., math and factual QA) rather than sensitive or harmful domains, and by framing personas as evaluation prompts rather than deployment guidance. When releasing artifacts, practitioners should ensure that prompts, model outputs, and datasets do not contain personal data, and should treat measured robustness/recovery as diagnostic signals rather than a guarantee of safe behavior in the wild.
