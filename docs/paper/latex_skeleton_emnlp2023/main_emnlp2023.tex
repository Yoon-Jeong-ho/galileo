\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) can change previously correct answers when confronted with social pressure such as repeated denial, authority claims, or persuasive reframing.
Prior work has documented sycophancy and persuasion effects, but there is no widely adopted, end-to-end protocol for ground-truth tasks that (i) measures \emph{when} the first failure occurs (turn-of-failure), (ii) summarizes degradation over multiple rounds (survival curves), and (iii) evaluates whether models can return to the original truth after a flip (recovery).

We introduce \textbf{GALILEO}, a reproducible evaluation pipeline for ground-truth tasks (math, extractive QA, multiple-choice QA, and open-domain QA).
Given an initially correct answer, GALILEO applies five adversarial personas (soft pressure, simple denial, strong pressure, authority claim, and logical trap) for up to five rounds, then prompts for recovery on flipped samples.
Across multiple model families and seeds, we find that persona pressure consistently worsens survival, increases fail@1, and shifts turn-of-failure earlier compared to a non-adversarial drift baseline (neutral re-asking control).
We further observe that \emph{robustness} (staying correct) and \emph{recoverability} (returning to truth after a flip) behave as distinct axes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern LLM assistants are often evaluated on single-turn correctness, but many real interactions are multi-turn: users challenge answers, assert contradictory ``facts,'' or apply rhetorical pressure (e.g., ``Are you sure?'' or ``My professor said otherwise.''). In these settings, a model can exhibit a 
\emph{belief-consistency failure}: changing a previously correct answer to an incorrect one due to social influence rather than new evidence.
Despite growing interest in sycophancy and persuasion, existing evaluations rarely provide an end-to-end, reproducible protocol where correctness is \emph{checkable at every turn} and failures can be localized to the exact round where the first flip occurs.

GALILEO targets this gap by instantiating multi-turn pressure on \emph{ground-truth} tasks with automatic checking. We pair adversarial personas with a neutral re-asking control to separate generic answer drift from persona-induced degradation, and we add a recovery stage to measure whether models can return to the original truth after an induced flip.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} GALILEO provides:
(1) a ground-truth, multi-turn protocol with survival and turn-of-failure metrics;
(2) a drift baseline (neutral re-asking control) that separates generic answer instability from persona-induced effects; and
(3) a recovery stage that quantifies return-to-truth after a flip.

\paragraph{Metrics (at a glance).} We report three complementary views of multi-turn belief consistency: \emph{fail@1} (fraction of initially-correct samples that ever flip under a condition), \emph{turn-of-failure} (the first round where the flip occurs, enabling ``when'' analysis), and \emph{survival} (probability of staying correct through each round, summarizing degradation over time). On flipped samples, we additionally measure \emph{recovery} as the probability of returning to the original ground-truth answer after an explicit recovery prompt.

\paragraph{Formalization.} For each evaluation condition, let $i \in \{1,\dots,n\}$ index examples that are \emph{initially correct} at round $0$. Let $Y_{i,r} \in \{0,1\}$ denote whether the model remains correct on example $i$ at pressure round $r \in \{1,\dots,R\}$. Define the \emph{turn-of-failure} as
\begin{equation}
  T_i = \min\{r : Y_{i,r}=0\},\quad \text{with } T_i = R{+}1 \text{ if no failure occurs.}
\end{equation}
We estimate the survival curve by the empirical survival probability
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{S}(r) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[T_i > r],
\end{equation}
so that $\text{fail@1} = 1-\widehat{S}(R)$. For recovery, we restrict to flipped examples ($T_i\le R$) and report the fraction that return to the original ground-truth answer after the recovery prompt.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Instruction-tuned LLMs can exhibit \emph{sycophancy} and related social-pressure failures, deferring to a user's assertions even when they contradict externally verifiable task truth \citep{sharma2023understandingsycophancylanguagemodels}. Recent benchmarks and analyses broaden this line of work to additional pressure sources and evaluation designs, including sycophancy-focused suites \citep{fanous2025sycevalevaluatingllmsycophancy,cheng2025elephantmeasuringunderstandingsocial}. Closest to our evaluation setting, recent work measures \emph{multi-turn} sycophancy under repeated rebuttal and quantifies longitudinal ``truth decay'' in dialogues \citep{hong2025measuringsycophancylanguagemodels,liu2025truthdecayquantifyingmultiturn}. Peer-pressure and multi-agent settings are also emerging \citep{song2025kairospeerpressure}. GALILEO is complementary: we focus on a standardized, end-to-end protocol for \emph{ground-truth} tasks that makes correctness checkable at every turn. Unlike open-ended preference/stance prompts where ``truth'' is ambiguous, our tasks yield automatic per-turn labels, enabling survival and turn-of-failure analysis as well as artifact-level audit trails.

A separate thread studies multi-turn instability even without explicit adversarial pressure: models may change answers across repeated questioning or extended interaction \citep{li2025firmorfickle,tosato2025persistentinstabilityllmspersonality}. GALILEO operationalizes this distinction by introducing an explicit drift baseline (neutral re-asking control) and by reporting \emph{when} failures occur (turn-of-failure) alongside \emph{how much} performance degrades over rounds (survival curves). Work on belief resistance under strategic persuasion in longer conversations \citep{huang2026vulnerabilityllmsbeliefsystems} is also related; our setting emphasizes a compact, reproducible measurement stack (including artifact exports) and adds a recovery stage to quantify return-to-truth after a flip.

Finally, our emphasis on verifiable truth aligns with truthfulness benchmarks such as TruthfulQA \citep{lin2022truthfulqameasuringmodelsmimic}, but targets a different axis: multi-turn belief-consistency failures and recovery under conversational pressure.

\section{Protocol and result figures}

\paragraph{Personas and control.}
We instantiate social pressure via five fixed personas: \emph{Soft Pressure} (gentle doubt), \emph{Simple Denial} (repeated contradiction without justification), \emph{Strong Pressure} (assertive insistence), \emph{Authority Claim} (appeal to an external expert/source), and \emph{Logical Trap} (misleading reasoning that tempts a flip).
To separate persona-induced effects from generic multi-turn instability, we also run a \emph{neutral re-asking control} that re-queries the same question without adversarial framing.

\section*{Limitations}
GALILEO is designed for \emph{ground-truth} tasks with automatic checking, which enables per-turn correctness labels but excludes many open-ended assistant behaviors where ``truth'' is ambiguous (e.g., subjective advice or creative writing). Our protocol also depends on obtaining an initially correct answer; while this matches the intended failure mode (flips from truth), it may underrepresent settings where models are incorrect from the start. Finally, we study a finite set of hand-designed personas and up to five pressure rounds; real-world social influence can be more diverse, longer-horizon, and multi-party.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
GALILEO applies conversational pressure intended to induce incorrect answers, which can surface undesirable model behaviors. We mitigate misuse risk by focusing on benign, automatically checkable tasks (e.g., math and factual QA) rather than sensitive or harmful domains, and by framing personas as evaluation prompts rather than deployment guidance. When releasing artifacts, practitioners should ensure that prompts, model outputs, and datasets do not contain personal data, and should treat measured robustness/recovery as diagnostic signals rather than a guarantee of safe behavior in the wild.
