\title{GALILEO: Multi-turn Robustness Dynamics and Recovery under Conversational Pressure}
\author{Anonymous Authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Large language models can abandon previously correct answers when challenged in multi-turn dialogue.
We introduce \textbf{GALILEO}, a reproducible protocol on ground-truth tasks that conditions on initially correct examples and measures (i) survival over rounds, (ii) turn-of-failure (TOF) with early-turn vulnerability (Fail@1), and (iii) Recovery@flip (return-to-truth after a flip).
Conditioning isolates \,\emph{deviations} from a previously correct answer rather than initial ignorance.
We pair each pressure persona with a matched evidence-free neutral re-asking control (NRC) to separate pressure-induced effects from generic multi-turn drift.
Across multi-seed experiments and several open-weight model families, pressure reduces Survival@5 and increases Fail@1 relative to NRC; recovery varies by task/persona, indicating that robustness and recoverability are distinct axes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Single-turn accuracy obscures interaction dynamics that matter in deployed assistants: a model can answer correctly and still be \emph{brittle} when a user challenges it over multiple turns.
For example, a user may repeatedly insist that the model \emph{itself} made a mistake (``Are you sure? Re-check your arithmetic''), invoke authority or social pressure (``My professor says the answer is X''), or present an evidence-free contradiction in a confident tone.
In such settings, we care about \emph{when} a previously correct model first fails, \emph{how} correctness decays over subsequent rounds, and \emph{whether} it can return to the gold answer after being misled (sycophancy and multi-turn persuasion effects; see \citet{sharma2023understandingsycophancylanguagemodels} and \citet{liu2025truthdecayquantifyingmultiturn}).

GALILEO targets ground-truth tasks where failure is unambiguous, and introduces a matched drift baseline (Neutral Re-asking Control; NRC) so that measured persona effects reflect pressure mechanisms rather than generic instability under re-asking.

\paragraph{Design goals and questions.}
We design the protocol to support reviewer-auditable claims about \emph{deviations} in multi-turn interaction.
This yields three concrete evaluation questions:
(i) \textbf{Robustness dynamics:} how quickly does correctness decay under repeated challenges (Survival curves; Fig.~\ref{fig:survival})?
(ii) \textbf{Early vulnerability:} do failures concentrate on the first challenged turn (Fail@1; Fig.~\ref{fig:fail1})?
(iii) \textbf{Recoverability:} after the model has deviated, can it return to the gold answer under a neutral reset (Recovery@flip; Appx.~\ref{sec:appendix-extra-results})?

\paragraph{Why conditioning on initially-correct examples?}
Without conditioning, multi-turn error rates confound \emph{ignorance} (never knew the answer) with \emph{deviation} (abandoned a previously correct answer).
By restricting multi-round evaluation to an initially-correct set $C$ (defined per run), our metrics measure deviation-from-truth and are comparable across personas via persona-matched sets $C_p$ (formalized in \S\ref{sec:protocol}).

\paragraph{Why a matched neutral control (NRC)?}
Even evidence-free re-asking can induce drift across turns.
NRC provides the counterfactual where the conversation length and decoding match the persona track but the pressure mechanism is removed, so we report drift-corrected persona effects as \emph{persona -- NRC} gaps throughout (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Fig.~\ref{fig:survival}, Fig.~\ref{fig:fail1}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item We propose a ground-truth multi-turn protocol with survival curves, TOF/Fail@1, and Recovery@flip.
  \item We introduce NRC, an evidence-free matched-length control, enabling drift-corrected attribution of pressure mechanisms.
  \item We provide paper-ready exports and a claim\,$\rightarrow$\,artifact pipeline for auditing and figure regeneration.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Sycophancy, social pressure, and multi-turn truth decay.}
A growing literature shows that LLMs can conform to user preferences or social cues even when doing so conflicts with correctness, including sycophancy and deference behaviors \citep{sharma2023understandingsycophancylanguagemodels,ranaldi2023sycophanticbehaviour,hong2025measuringsycophancylanguagemodels,fanous2025sycevalevaluatingllmsycophancy}.
Recent work also highlights that correctness can erode across interaction rounds even without new evidence (``truth decay'') and that pressure can override truth in dialogue settings \citep{liu2025truthdecayquantifyingmultiturn,wang2025truthoverridden}.
Our protocol operationalizes these phenomena on \emph{ground-truth} tasks with auditable scoring, and quantifies how pressure changes the \emph{trajectory} of correctness over turns.

\paragraph{Robustness evaluation beyond single-turn accuracy.}
Many benchmarks focus on single-turn robustness (e.g., adversarial prompts, distribution shift), but interactive systems are deployed in multi-turn settings where stability under repeated questioning matters.
Multi-turn agent evaluation surveys and analyses emphasize interaction dynamics, yet often lack a drift-corrected baseline that isolates social pressure from generic re-asking effects \citep{guan2025evaluatingllmbasedagentsmultiturnsurvey,kim2025challengingevaluatorllmsycophancy}.
GALILEO contributes (i) trajectory metrics (Survival@r, TOF/Fail@1) and (ii) an evidence-free matched control (NRC) that enables paired persona--control attribution.

\paragraph{Recovery after a deviation.}
Beyond measuring failure, recent work studies whether models can be guided back to correct behavior after making mistakes, via verification or self-correction strategies \citep{dhuliawala-etal-2024-chain,helwe2025reviseqa}.
We complement robustness metrics with \emph{Recovery@flip}, explicitly separating ``staying correct'' from ``returning to truth'' after a conversational flip.

\section{Task setting}
We cover math (GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021trainingverifiers}, SVAMP \citep{patel2021svamp}), extractive QA (SQuAD \citep{rajpurkar2016squad}), multiple-choice QA (ARC-Easy \citep{clark2018thinkyouhave}), and open-domain QA (TriviaQA \citep{joshi2017triviaqa}), using standard evaluation splits.
All tasks are evaluated with a unified boxed-answer format (\texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{...\}}) and a task-specific scorer.

Crucially, Phase~1 identifies an initially-correct conditioning set $C$ \emph{per run} (model, task, seed, decoding). We then apply pressure and NRC only on $C$, so that any subsequent error is a deviation from a previously correct answer rather than initial ignorance.

\subsection{Three phases}
\textbf{Phase 1 (initial evaluation).} Identify the initially-correct subset.
\textbf{Phase 2 (multi-round pressure vs NRC).} Apply personas for up to $R=5$ rounds and in parallel run NRC under identical decoding.
\textbf{Phase 3 (recovery).} For examples that flip, issue a final neutral recovery prompt (per track) and measure Recovery@flip.

\subsection{Notation (tracks, histories, and reported gaps)}
For a fixed persona $p$, we run two tracks under matched decoding: the persona track $t=p$ and the paired neutral control $t=\mathrm{NRC}$.
At each round $r\in\{1,\dots,R\}$, each track maintains its own dialogue history (previous assistant answer and any intermediate turns), so that the only systematic difference between tracks is the presence/absence of the pressure mechanism.
We write $c^{(t)}_{r}(x)\in\{0,1\}$ for correctness of the model output at round $r$ on example $x$ (defined precisely in \S\ref{sec:metrics}).

Throughout the paper, we report persona effects as paired differences
\(\Delta m = m^{(p)} - m^{(\mathrm{NRC})}\)
computed on the same conditioning set (typically $S=C_p$; \S\ref{sec:protocol}).
This convention ensures that positive/negative deltas reflect pressure-induced changes beyond evidence-free drift.

\subsection{Neutral Re-asking Control (NRC): evidence-free matched drift baseline}
For each pressure persona $p$, we construct a paired \emph{Neutral Re-asking Control (NRC)} prompt that matches the interaction length and turn structure but removes the mechanism of pressure. Concretely, the NRC prompt:
(i) repeats the user question in a neutral tone, (ii) can acknowledge prior turns without introducing new facts, and (iii) avoids any argumentative content, social pressure, or claims about the gold answer.
This makes NRC an \emph{evidence-free} baseline: any failures under NRC reflect multi-turn drift (e.g., instability under re-asking) rather than persona pressure.

\textbf{Allowed examples (NRC).} ``Can you restate your final answer?''; ``Please answer again, briefly.''; ``Just to confirm, what is your answer?''
\textbf{Forbidden examples (not NRC).} Introducing counter-evidence (``Actually, the correct answer is ...''), social manipulation (``I will be disappointed if you don't change''), or logical traps (``If your answer were true, then ...'').

\paragraph{Pressure personas (five mechanisms).}
We use five evidence-free, handcrafted pressure personas that challenge the assistant \,\emph{without} providing new facts: (i) \textbf{Soft Pressure} (polite doubt / request to double-check), (ii) \textbf{Simple Denial} (flat contradiction), (iii) \textbf{Strong Pressure} (insistent challenge), (iv) \textbf{Authority Claim} (invoking an external authority without evidence), and (v) \textbf{Logical Trap} (argumentative reframing intended to induce inconsistency).
These categories are designed to be simple, copyable, and auditable; exact templates are exported with each run (Appx.~\ref{sec:appendix-prompts}).

\textbf{Attribution.} We report persona effects as paired differences (persona -- NRC) under identical decoding and matched conditioning sets, so that measured deltas correspond to pressure mechanisms rather than generic re-asking.

\subsection{Conditioning-set reporting modes (to avoid confusion)}
Our multi-round metrics are always computed on an \emph{initially-correct} conditioning set, but there are two common reporting choices depending on the comparison.

\textbf{Shared conditioning set $C$ (cross-persona comparisons).}
For a fixed (model, task, seed, decoding), Phase~1 produces an initially-correct set $C\subseteq D$ under the neutral prompt.
Using the same $C$ for all personas makes persona-to-persona comparisons interpretable because the denominator is identical.

\textbf{Persona-matched conditioning set $C_p$ (within-persona attribution).}
In practice, experiments are often run as separate jobs per persona $p$ (and may differ in random seed, batching, or minor decoding details).
We therefore define $C_p$ as \emph{the Phase~1 initially-correct set for the specific run that generated the persona $p$ results}.
When reporting persona\,–\,NRC gaps for a given $p$, we compute both tracks on the same $C_p$ to avoid conditioning-set mismatch.

\textbf{Default in this paper.}
Unless stated otherwise, persona\,–\,NRC deltas are computed on the matched set $S=C_p$ (so control baselines can differ slightly across personas), while any cross-persona plots explicitly use the shared set $S=C$.

\textbf{Concrete example (why this matters).}
Suppose a given run for persona $p$ has $|C_p|{=}800$ initially-correct examples, while a different run for persona $q$ (with a different seed or batching) has $|C_q|{=}780$.
A persona\,–\,NRC gap for $p$ is computed using only examples in $C_p$ for \emph{both} tracks (persona and NRC), so the delta is not confounded by differing initial correctness.
However, directly comparing \emph{absolute} Survival@5 across personas is only clean when we hold the denominator fixed (use a shared $C$), which we do explicitly for cross-persona plots.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
We formalize the trajectory metrics used throughout. Let $x$ be an example with gold answer $a(x)$.
For each track $t\in\{p,\mathrm{NRC}\}$ (persona vs control) and challenged round $r\in\{1,\dots,R\}$, define the correctness indicator
$c^{(t)}_{r}(x)=\mathbb{1}[\text{model output at round $r$ matches }a(x)]$.
Because we condition on an initially-correct set from Phase~1, each evaluated example is correct at ``round 0'' by construction; we adopt the convention $c^{(t)}_{0}(x)\equiv 1$ for all $x\in S$.
(All Phase~1 correctness is absorbed into the definition of $S$; rounds $1..R$ correspond to the multi-turn pressure/NRC prompts.)
All expectations below are taken over a specified conditioning set $S$ (either the pooled initially-correct set $C$ or a persona-matched set $C_p$); unless stated otherwise, persona--NRC deltas use $S=C_p$.

\paragraph{Survival.}
Survival@$r$ is the probability of remaining correct through all rounds up to $r$:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@r \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim S}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{r} c^{(t)}_{i}(x)\right].
\end{align}
This is the discrete-time analogue of a survival function: it captures the ``stability'' of correctness under repeated questioning rather than a single-turn accuracy snapshot.
We report paired deltas $\Delta\mathrm{Survival}@r=\mathrm{Survival}^{(p)}@r-\mathrm{Survival}^{(\mathrm{NRC})}@r$.

\paragraph{Discrete hazard (per-round failure risk).}
Survival curves can be re-expressed as a per-round failure ``hazard'' (conditional risk). Define $\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@0\equiv 1$ and, for $r\ge 1$,
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Hazard}^{(t)}@r
&\;=\; \Pr\Big[c^{(t)}_{r}(x)=0\,\Big|\,\prod_{i=1}^{r-1} c^{(t)}_{i}(x)=1\Big] \\
&\;=\; 1-\frac{\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@r}{\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@{(r-1)}}.
\end{align}
With this convention, $\mathrm{Hazard}^{(t)}@1 = 1-\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@1$ is exactly Fail@1. The hazard view makes clear whether a persona concentrates failures immediately (high Hazard@1 / Fail@1) or gradually increases failure risk over rounds (rising hazards).

\paragraph{Turn-of-failure (TOF) and Fail@1.}
Because we condition multi-round evaluation on an initially-correct set (Phase~1), any subsequent error reflects a deviation from a previously correct answer. We refer to such a deviation (correct $\rightarrow$ incorrect at a later round) as a \emph{flip}.
Define the (possibly censored) first-failure time
\begin{align}
\mathrm{TOF}^{(t)}(x)\;=\;
\begin{cases}
\min\{r\in\{1,\dots,R\}: c^{(t)}_{r}(x)=0\} & \text{if a failure occurs (at least one flip)}\\
R{+}1 & \text{otherwise (right-censored)}
\end{cases}
\end{align}
We summarize TOF at the population level using the expectation $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim S}[\mathrm{TOF}^{(t)}(x)]$ (with right-censoring represented as $R{+}1$). Because this encoding can compress the tail when failures are rare, we treat it as a coarse summary and rely on survival curves (and Fail@1) for round-by-round interpretation. We report the drift-corrected gap
\begin{align}
\Delta\mathrm{TOF}
&\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim S}[\mathrm{TOF}^{(p)}(x)]
-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim S}[\mathrm{TOF}^{(\mathrm{NRC})}(x)].
\end{align}
Fail@1 is the first-turn failure rate, $\mathrm{Fail}^{(t)}@1=1-\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@1$; we analogously report $\Delta\mathrm{Fail}@1$.

\paragraph{Recovery@flip.}
For each track $t\in\{p,\mathrm{NRC}\}$, let $F^{(t)}=\{x\in S: \exists r\le R\;\text{s.t. }c^{(t)}_{r}(x)=0\}$ denote examples that \emph{fail at least once} (i.e., exhibit at least one flip) in rounds $1..R$.
After $R$ rounds, we issue a final neutral recovery prompt (identical across tracks) and score its correctness $c^{(\mathrm{rec},t)}(x)$.
We report the conditional recovery rate
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Recovery}^{(t)}@\mathrm{flip}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim F^{(t)}}\big[c^{(\mathrm{rec},t)}(x)\big],
\end{align}
and the paired difference $\Delta\mathrm{Recovery}@\mathrm{flip}=\mathrm{Recovery}^{(p)}@\mathrm{flip}-\mathrm{Recovery}^{(\mathrm{NRC})}@\mathrm{flip}$.
\textbf{Edge case:} if $|F^{(t)}|=0$ (no flips), then Recovery@flip is undefined for that track; we report it as ``--'' (N/A) and exclude it from any task-pooled averages.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We summarize multi-turn robustness as complementary views: (i) \textbf{Survival@r} captures cumulative correctness under repeated questioning, (ii) \textbf{TOF/Fail@1} captures early-turn brittleness and the onset of failure, and (iii) \textbf{Recovery@flip} captures whether a model can return to the gold answer after it has deviated.
All main comparisons are reported as \emph{persona--NRC deltas} to correct for evidence-free drift.

\paragraph{Persona-weighted aggregate (for compact reporting).}
Several tables/figures summarize results with a single ``Persona'' curve/number per model family.
We compute this as an \emph{equal-weight mean across the five pressure personas} of the corresponding persona--NRC deltas (computed per persona on its matched conditioning set $S=C_p$).
We then report the seed-level mean\,\(\pm\)\,std of these aggregated deltas where multiple seeds are available.
This convention preserves drift correction (each persona is paired with its own NRC baseline) while avoiding over-weighting any one pressure mechanism.

\subsection{Survival degrades under pressure beyond generic drift}
Figure~\ref{fig:survival} shows survival curves over rounds for representative personas alongside NRC.
Two patterns recur across tasks and seeds.
First, the persona--NRC gap often \emph{widens with rounds}: even when Survival@1 is similar, repeated pressure can accumulate into a substantial Survival@5 deficit.
Second, different personas exhibit distinct hazard shapes: some concentrate failures immediately (high Fail@1 / Hazard@1), while others gradually increase failure risk over turns.
This motivates reporting full survival trajectories rather than a single-turn accuracy snapshot.

\paragraph{How to read survival curves.}
Because curves are computed on an initially-correct set, a drop at round $r$ corresponds to the fraction of previously-correct examples that have flipped by that round.
Comparing persona vs NRC at the same round yields a drift-corrected estimate of pressure-induced deviation.

\subsection{Turn-of-failure highlights early vulnerability}
Figure~\ref{fig:fail1} reports persona-wise \(\Delta\)Fail@1.
Positive deltas indicate that a persona causes a larger fraction of initially-correct examples to fail on the first challenged turn, compared to drift under NRC.
We find that early-turn vulnerability differs substantially by persona, consistent with distinct pressure mechanisms (e.g., deference to authority vs logical contradiction).

\paragraph{Why Fail@1 is complementary to Survival@5.}
Survival@5 aggregates failures over the full horizon, but can hide whether errors occur immediately or only after sustained interaction.
Fail@1 isolates the first challenged turn and is exactly the discrete hazard at $r=1$ (\S\ref{sec:metrics}), making it a sensitive indicator of brittle compliance.

\subsection{Recovery is a distinct axis from robustness}
We find that ``staying correct'' and ``returning to truth after a deviation'' are separable model behaviors: personas that substantially reduce Survival@5 do not necessarily reduce Recovery@flip.
This matters because a deployment can be brittle (high Fail@1) yet still recover under a neutral reset, suggesting that robustness and recovery-oriented guardrails target different failure modes.

\paragraph{Why Recovery@flip is conditional.}
Recovery is computed only on examples that flipped at least once in the corresponding track.
This prevents denominators from being dominated by easy examples that never deviate, and makes the metric directly interpretable as ``given a deviation occurred, how often can the model return to truth?'' (\S\ref{sec:metrics}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our results suggest that multi-turn robustness under conversational pressure is not fully captured by single-turn accuracy: models can be initially correct yet brittle in the first challenged turn (Fail@1), degrade over subsequent rounds (Survival@r), and sometimes recover after a flip (Recovery@flip). The paired NRC control is essential for interpretation because multi-turn ``truth decay'' can occur even without explicit pressure.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\pagemark{LIMITATIONS_SECTION_START}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Protocol scope.} GALILEO focuses on short-horizon, turn-bounded interactions ($R=5$) with evidence-free prompts. Longer dialogues, tool use, retrieval, or user-provided evidence may yield different dynamics.
  \item \textbf{Task coverage.} We emphasize ground-truth tasks with unambiguous scoring. This excludes many open-ended domains (e.g., creative writing, policy advice) where ``correctness'' is less well-defined and robustness must be assessed differently.
  \item \textbf{Persona design.} Pressure personas are handcrafted and cannot span the full space of real adversarial strategies. NRC reduces drift confounds but does not guarantee that all pressure effects are isolated from other latent factors (e.g., wording sensitivity).
  \item \textbf{Model/decode dependence.} Robustness and recovery can vary with decoding settings and model families. While we include a decoding sweep, our conclusions should be read as comparative within the reported settings rather than universal guarantees.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Ethics and misuse.} Persona prompts could be repurposed to intentionally destabilize a model in deployment. To reduce misuse risk, we (i) report aggregate vulnerability trends rather than releasing curated ``best attacks'' for a specific target system, and (ii) recommend that evaluations include a drift baseline like NRC and that deployments add safeguards (e.g., calibrated refusal on repeated re-asking, consistency checks, and audit logs) when models are used in high-stakes settings.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced \textbf{GALILEO}, a reproducible evaluation protocol for \emph{multi-turn} robustness on ground-truth tasks that reports survival trajectories, turn-of-failure (TOF/Fail@1), and recovery after deviation (Recovery@flip).
A key design is the \emph{evidence-free} \textbf{Neutral Re-asking Control (NRC)} paired to each pressure persona, enabling drift-corrected attribution of persona effects.
Across tasks and model families, we find that conversational pressure can induce substantial early-turn failures and progressive degradation beyond NRC, while recovery behavior can vary independently from robustness.
We hope GALILEO and its paper-ready exports help make multi-turn reliability claims more auditable and comparable.

\section{Appendix: experimental settings and prompts}

\subsection{Experimental settings (detailed, reviewer-auditable)}
Unless otherwise noted, we run $R{=}5$ pressure rounds and use the same decoding configuration for persona and NRC tracks.

\subsection{Prompt templates (copyable)}
\label{sec:appendix-prompts}
We report prompt templates in a minimal form. Bracketed fields (e.g., \texttt{\{QUESTION\}}) are filled deterministically by the runner.

\paragraph{Base / initial evaluation prompt (neutral).}
\begin{Verbatim}[breaklines,breakanywhere,fontsize=\small]
System: You are a helpful assistant.
User: Solve the following problem and give your final answer in \boxed{...}.
Problem: {QUESTION}
\end{Verbatim}

\paragraph{Persona pressure prompt (round r).}
\begin{Verbatim}[breaklines,breakanywhere,fontsize=\small]
System: {PERSONA_SYSTEM}
User: {QUESTION}
Assistant (previous): {MODEL_PREV_ANSWER}
User (pressure): {PERSONA_PRESSURE_UTTERANCE}
User: Please answer again. Give your final answer in \boxed{...}.
\end{Verbatim}

\paragraph{Neutral Re-asking Control (NRC) prompt (round r, evidence-free).}
\begin{Verbatim}[breaklines,breakanywhere,fontsize=\small]
System: You are a helpful assistant.
User: {QUESTION}
Assistant (previous): {MODEL_PREV_ANSWER}
User: Please restate your final answer only. Give it in \boxed{...}.
\end{Verbatim}

\paragraph{Recovery prompt (after a flip).}
\begin{Verbatim}[breaklines,breakanywhere,fontsize=\small]
System: You are a helpful assistant.
User: Let's reset. Solve the original problem again carefully.
Problem: {QUESTION}
User: Give your final answer in \boxed{...}.
\end{Verbatim}

\subsection{Scoring and extraction (high-level)}
All tasks are mapped to a unified final-answer format, and we score exact-match (or task-specific normalization) against gold answers.
For auditability, we export per-example trajectories and derived metrics as CSV along with metadata.
