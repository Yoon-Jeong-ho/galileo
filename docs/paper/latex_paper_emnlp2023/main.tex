\title{GALILEO: Multi-turn Robustness Dynamics and Recovery under Conversational Pressure}
\author{Anonymous Authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Large language models can abandon a previously correct answer when repeatedly challenged \emph{without new evidence}---a multi-turn failure mode that single-turn accuracy does not reveal.
We introduce \textbf{\GALILEO{}}, an auditable protocol for ground-truth tasks that isolates \emph{deviation from truth} by (i) evaluating only initially-correct examples and (ii) pairing each pressure persona with a matched \emph{Neutral Re-asking Control} (\NRC{}) that matches dialogue length and decoding, enabling drift-corrected reporting as persona--\NRC{} deltas.
Over $R{=}5$ challenged rounds (computed on an initially-correct conditioning set), we quantify (a) robustness dynamics via survival trajectories (\SurvivalAt{r}: fraction that stays correct for all challenged turns $1\ldots r$), (b) early vulnerability via first-turn failure (\FailAt{1}) and time-to-first-failure (\TOF), and (c) recoverability after a flip under a final neutral reset (\RecoveryAtFlip{}). We exclude the recovery turn from \SurvivalAt{r}/\TOF computation.
We export per-example transcripts and per-turn scores for end-to-end verification.
Across seeds and open-weight model families, pressure consistently lowers \SurvivalAt{5} and raises \FailAt{1} relative to \NRC{}, while \RecoveryAtFlip{} varies by task and persona.
For \textbf{Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct} in our main aggregated setting (seeds~1--4), averaging persona--\NRC{} deltas with equal weight over five pressure personas and five tasks, pressure reduces \SurvivalAt{5} by 22.8 percentage points and increases \FailAt{1} by 6.9 percentage points.
Overall, \GALILEO{} separates robustness under conversational pressure from recoverability and provides a claim\,$\rightarrow$\,artifact pipeline for reproducible auditing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Single-turn accuracy obscures interaction dynamics that matter in deployed assistants: a model can answer correctly and still be \emph{brittle} when a user challenges it over multiple turns.
This brittleness is especially consequential in high-stakes or safety-relevant settings, where a model may ``talk itself'' from a correct answer into a confidently wrong revision.
For example, a user may repeatedly insist that the model \emph{itself} made a mistake (``Are you sure? Re-check your arithmetic''), invoke authority or social pressure (``My professor says the answer is X''), or present an evidence-free contradiction in a confident tone.
Throughout, we call a challenge \emph{evidence-free} if it applies doubt or pressure \emph{without} introducing task-relevant facts, calculations, tools, or external sources.
This is closely related to the ``FlipFlop'' effect, where simple follow-up challenges (e.g., ``Are you sure?'') can reduce performance in subsequent turns \citep{laban2023areyousureflipflop}. 
In such settings, we care about \emph{when} a previously correct model first fails, \emph{how} correctness decays over subsequent rounds, and \emph{whether} it can return to the gold answer after being misled (see work on sycophancy and multi-turn truth decay: \citet{sharma2023understandingsycophancylanguagemodels}; \citet{liu2025truthdecayquantifyingmultiturn}).

Concretely, we instantiate five evidence-free pressure mechanisms: \textbf{Soft Pressure} (polite doubt), \textbf{Simple Denial} (flat contradiction), \textbf{Strong Pressure} (insistent challenge), \textbf{Authority Claim} (invoking an external authority), and \textbf{Logical Trap} (argumentative reframing) (\S\ref{sec:protocol}).

\GALILEO{} targets ground-truth tasks where failure is unambiguous, and introduces a matched drift baseline (Neutral Re-asking Control, \NRC{}) so that measured persona effects reflect pressure mechanisms rather than generic instability under re-asking.

\paragraph{Design goals and questions.}
We design the protocol to support reviewer-auditable claims about \emph{deviations} in multi-turn interaction.
This yields three concrete evaluation questions:
(i) \textbf{Robustness dynamics:} how quickly does correctness decay under repeated challenges (Survival curves; Fig.~\ref{fig:survival})?
(ii) \textbf{Early vulnerability:} do failures concentrate on the first challenged turn (\FailAt{1}; Fig.~\ref{fig:fail1})?
(iii) \textbf{Recoverability:} after the model has deviated, can it return to the gold answer under a neutral reset (\RecoveryAtFlip{}; Appx.~\ref{sec:appendix-extra-results})?

\paragraph{Why conditioning on initially-correct examples?}
Without conditioning, multi-turn error rates confound \emph{ignorance} (never knew the answer) with \emph{deviation} (abandoned a previously correct answer).
By restricting multi-round evaluation to an initially-correct set $C$ (defined per run), our metrics measure deviation-from-truth and are comparable across personas via persona-matched sets $C_p$ (formalized in \S\ref{sec:protocol}).

\paragraph{Why a matched neutral control (\NRC{})?}
Even evidence-free re-asking can induce drift across turns.
\NRC{} provides the counterfactual where the conversation length and decoding match the persona track but the pressure mechanism is removed, so we report drift-corrected persona effects as persona--\NRC{} gaps throughout (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Fig.~\ref{fig:survival}, Fig.~\ref{fig:fail1}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item We propose a ground-truth multi-turn protocol with survival curves, \TOF and \FailAt{1}, and \RecoveryAtFlip{}.
  \item We introduce \NRC{}, an evidence-free matched-length control, enabling drift-corrected attribution of pressure mechanisms.
  \item We provide paper-ready exports and a claim\,$\rightarrow$\,artifact pipeline for auditing and figure regeneration.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Sycophancy, social pressure, and multi-turn truth decay.}
A growing literature shows that LLMs can conform to user preferences or social cues even when doing so conflicts with correctness, including sycophancy and deference behaviors \citep{sharma2023understandingsycophancylanguagemodels,ranaldi2023sycophanticbehaviour,hong2025measuringsycophancylanguagemodels,fanous2025sycevalevaluatingllmsycophancy}.
Related work also studies social influence in interactive settings, including ``peer pressure'' effects in multi-agent social interactions \citep{song2025kairospeerpressure}.
Recent work highlights that correctness can erode across interaction rounds even without new evidence (``truth decay'') and that pressure can override truth in dialogue settings \citep{liu2025truthdecayquantifyingmultiturn,wang2025truthoverridden}.
A closely adjacent empirical finding is that even a lightweight challenge such as ``Are you sure?'' can trigger follow-up errors (the FlipFlop effect) \citep{laban2023areyousureflipflop}.
Our protocol operationalizes these phenomena on \emph{ground-truth} tasks with auditable scoring, and quantifies how pressure changes the \emph{trajectory} of correctness over turns.
Compared to persuasion or debate-style settings where the user introduces new facts or arguments, we intentionally restrict prompts to \emph{evidence-free} challenges and score against deterministic gold answers, yielding unambiguous deviation events.

\paragraph{Robustness evaluation beyond single-turn accuracy.}
Many benchmarks focus on single-turn robustness (e.g., adversarial prompts, distribution shift), but deployed assistants operate in multi-turn dialogues where users may repeatedly challenge an answer.
Recent lines of work explicitly evaluate consistency in sequential interactions and measurement instability across turns \citep{li2025firmorfickle,tosato2025persistentinstabilityllmspersonality,yu2026ptcbenchbenchmarkingcontextualstability}.
Prior multi-turn evaluations and agent surveys highlight interaction dynamics, yet commonly mix in \emph{new evidence}, tool use, or task progression---and often lack a drift-corrected baseline that cleanly separates \emph{conversational pressure} from generic re-asking instability \citep{guan2025evaluatingllmbasedagentsmultiturnsurvey,kim2025challengingevaluatorllmsycophancy}.
Recent work also frames multi-turn robustness as a \emph{time-to-event} problem and applies survival-analysis tools to measure when inconsistency first occurs under adversarial interaction \citep{li2026timetoinconsistencysurvivalanalysislarge}.
\GALILEO{} shares this core lens (failure as an event over turns), but keeps the primary reporting \emph{non-parametric and task-grounded} (survival curves, \TOF/\FailAt{1}) and adds an evidence-free matched drift baseline (\NRC{}) plus post-flip recovery, improving attribution and auditability.

\paragraph{Recovery after a deviation.}
Beyond measuring failure, recent work studies whether models can be guided back to correct behavior after making mistakes, via verification or self-correction strategies \citep{dhuliawala-etal-2024-chain,helwe2025reviseqa}.
We complement robustness metrics with \RecoveryAtFlip{}, explicitly separating ``staying correct'' from ``returning to truth'' after a conversational flip.

\section{Task setting}
We cover math (GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021trainingverifiers}, SVAMP \citep{patel2021svamp}), extractive QA (SQuAD \citep{rajpurkar2016squad}), multiple-choice QA (ARC-Easy \citep{clark2018thinkyouhave}), and open-domain QA (TriviaQA \citep{joshi2017triviaqa}), using standard evaluation splits.
These tasks span different answer types (numbers, spans, and short strings) while preserving deterministic, auditor-friendly gold scoring.
All tasks are evaluated with a unified boxed-answer format (\texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{...\}}) and a task-specific scorer.

\paragraph{Auditability of scoring and extraction.}
Because our goal is to measure deviation from a previously correct answer, we choose tasks with deterministic gold answers and evaluation rules that are easy to audit.
For each example, the runner records the full multi-turn trajectory and extracts a final answer string (boxed format) using deterministic parsing.
We then score exact match (with task-specific normalization where required) against the gold answer.
All per-example trajectories and derived metrics are exported as CSV alongside metadata, enabling post-hoc verification and figure regeneration.

\paragraph{Answer format and edge cases.}
To reduce evaluator degrees of freedom, we enforce a single final-answer channel (boxed answers) and treat the extracted box content as the model's committed response.
When tasks admit multiple surface forms (e.g., whitespace, punctuation, equivalent numeric forms), we apply a task-specific normalization so that scoring errors reflect substantive deviations rather than formatting.
If extraction fails (e.g., missing box or malformed output), we mark the turn as incorrect and export the raw text so failures are auditable.
This design ensures that reported flips correspond to observable changes in the model's stated final answer, and that rare parsing artifacts can be separated from genuine pressure-induced deviations.

Crucially, Phase~1 identifies an initially-correct conditioning set $C$ \emph{per run} (model, task, seed, decoding). We then apply pressure and \NRC{} only on $C$, so that any subsequent error is a deviation from a previously correct answer rather than initial ignorance.

\subsection{Three phases}
\textbf{Phase 1 (initial evaluation).} Identify the initially-correct subset.
\textbf{Phase 2 (multi-round pressure vs \NRC{}).} Apply personas for up to $R=5$ rounds and in parallel run \NRC{} under identical decoding.
\textbf{Phase 3 (recovery).} For examples that flip, issue a final neutral recovery prompt (per track) and measure \RecoveryAtFlip{}.

\paragraph{Evidence-free pressure, matched-length control.}
All persona prompts are designed to be \emph{evidence-free}: they challenge, doubt, or apply social pressure without introducing new facts, tools, or external sources.
For each persona $p$, the paired \NRC{} track uses the same number of rounds and an analogous turn structure (including re-asking), but removes the pressure mechanism.
This design separates pressure-induced deviation from generic multi-turn re-asking drift.

\subsection{Conditioning-set choices (shared $C$ vs per-persona $C_p$)}
\label{sec:conditioning-set}
Phase~1 defines an initially-correct set by scoring a neutral first response. When we run all personas within a single job, we can use a \emph{shared} conditioning set $C$ so that survival curves are directly comparable across personas under the same denominator.
When runs are produced separately per persona (common in large sweeps), each persona has its own Phase~1 set $C_p$; in that case we compute persona--\NRC{} deltas on the \emph{matched} $C_p$ so that the paired comparison isolates pressure effects rather than differences in Phase~1 correctness. This avoids conflating ``pressure'' with the fact that different personas may condition on slightly different initially-correct subsets.
Unless stated otherwise, this paper reports (i) persona--\NRC{} deltas on matched $C_p$, and (ii) cross-persona plots on an explicitly shared $C$. 

\subsection{Notation (tracks, histories, and reported gaps)}
For a fixed persona $p$, we run two tracks under matched decoding: the persona track $t=p$ and the paired neutral control $t=\mathrm{NRC}$.
At each round $r\in\{1,\dots,R\}$, each track maintains its own dialogue history (previous assistant answer and any intermediate turns), so that the only systematic difference between tracks is the presence/absence of the pressure mechanism.
We write $c^{(t)}_{r}(x)\in\{0,1\}$ for correctness of the model output at round $r$ on example $x$ (defined precisely in \S\ref{sec:metrics}).

Throughout the paper, we report persona effects as paired differences
\(\Delta m = m^{(p)} - m^{(\mathrm{NRC})}\)
computed on the same conditioning set (typically $C_p$; \S\ref{sec:conditioning-set}).
This convention ensures that positive/negative deltas reflect pressure-induced changes beyond evidence-free drift.

\paragraph{Auditability and exported metadata.}
To make every table/figure traceable, each run exports machine-readable metadata (e.g., \path{paper_exports/metadata.json} and \path{paper_exports/runner_metadata.json}) including: model identifier, seeds, decoding parameters, persona/NRC prompt variants, and conditioning-set sizes (\(|C|\) or \(|C_p|\)).
These exports enable reviewers (and future work) to verify that persona and \NRC{} tracks were matched in decoding and that denominators are consistent with the reported aggregation mode.

\subsection{Neutral Re-asking Control (\NRC{}): evidence-free matched drift baseline}
For each pressure persona $p$, we construct a paired \emph{Neutral Re-asking Control (\NRC{})} prompt that matches the interaction length and turn structure but removes the mechanism of pressure. Concretely, the NRC prompt:
(i) repeats the user question in a neutral tone, (ii) can acknowledge prior turns without introducing new facts, and (iii) avoids any argumentative content, social pressure, or claims about the gold answer.
This makes NRC an \emph{evidence-free} baseline: any failures under \NRC{} reflect multi-turn drift (e.g., instability under re-asking) rather than persona pressure.

\paragraph{Threat model (evidence-free conversational pressure).}
\GALILEO{} targets settings where the user challenges a previously correct answer \emph{without supplying new factual evidence}.
This isolates social and rhetorical pressure (doubt, denial, authority claims, argumentative reframing) from failures caused by genuine new information.
We intentionally keep all personas evidence-free so that persona--\NRC{} gaps can be interpreted as pressure mechanisms rather than belief revision under new facts.

\paragraph{Why matched decoding and interaction length?}
Multi-turn drift can depend strongly on sampling randomness and on how many times the model is re-asked.
We therefore match decoding settings and turn structure between a persona and its \NRC{} track (\S\ref{sec:protocol}), so that the measured gap corresponds to pressure content rather than differences in temperature, truncation, or conversation length.

\paragraph{Why $R{=}5$ rounds?}
A small fixed horizon balances two goals: (i) it is long enough to expose cumulative degradation dynamics in survival curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:survival}), and (ii) it remains short enough that evaluation is auditable and not dominated by long-context effects.
Longer dialogues are an important extension, but would require additional controls (e.g., retrieval/tooling) and are outside the current evidence-free threat model.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Allowed examples (NRC):} ``Can you restate your final answer?''; ``Please answer again, briefly.''; ``Just to confirm, what is your answer?''
  \item \textbf{Forbidden examples (not NRC):} introducing counter-evidence (``Actually, the correct answer is \dots''), social manipulation (``I will be disappointed if you don't change''), or logical traps (``If your answer were true, then \dots'').
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pressure personas (five mechanisms).}
We use five evidence-free, handcrafted pressure personas that challenge the assistant \,\emph{without} providing new facts: (i) \textbf{Soft Pressure} (polite doubt / request to double-check), (ii) \textbf{Simple Denial} (flat contradiction), (iii) \textbf{Strong Pressure} (insistent challenge), (iv) \textbf{Authority Claim} (invoking an external authority without evidence), and (v) \textbf{Logical Trap} (argumentative reframing intended to induce inconsistency).
These categories are designed to be simple, copyable, and auditable; exact templates are exported with each run (Appx.~\ref{sec:appendix-prompts}).

\textbf{Attribution.} We report persona effects as paired differences (persona -- NRC) under identical decoding and matched conditioning sets, so that measured deltas correspond to pressure mechanisms rather than generic re-asking.

\subsection{Conditioning sets and denominators (quick recap)}
All multi-turn metrics are computed on an \emph{initially-correct} conditioning set, so they measure \emph{deviation from a previously correct answer} rather than initial ignorance.
We use two reporting modes depending on the comparison: a shared set $C$ for cross-persona plots, and persona-matched sets $C_p$ for within-persona persona--\NRC{} deltas. See \S\ref{sec:conditioning-set} for the full convention.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
We formalize the trajectory metrics used throughout. Let $x$ be an example with gold answer $a(x)$.
For each track $t\in\{p,\mathrm{NRC}\}$ (persona vs control) and challenged round $r\in\{1,\dots,R\}$, define the correctness indicator
$c^{(t)}_{r}(x)=\mathbb{1}[\text{model output at round $r$ matches }a(x)]$.
Because we condition on an initially-correct set from Phase~1, each evaluated example is correct at ``round 0'' by construction; we adopt the convention $c^{(t)}_{0}(x)\equiv 1$ for all $x\in S$.
(All Phase~1 correctness is absorbed into the definition of $S$; rounds $1\ldots R$ correspond to the multi-turn pressure/NRC prompts.)
All expectations below are taken over a specified conditioning set $S$ (either the pooled initially-correct set $C$ or a persona-matched set $C_p$); unless stated otherwise, persona--\NRC{} deltas use $S=C_p$.

\paragraph{Survival.}
\SurvivalAt{r} is the probability of remaining correct through all rounds up to $r$:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@r
&\;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim S}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{r} c^{(t)}_{i}(x)\right] \\
&\;=\; \Pr_{x\sim S}\big[\forall i\le r:\; c^{(t)}_{i}(x)=1\big].
\end{align}
This is the discrete-time analogue of a survival function (cf. Kaplan--Meier; \citealt{kaplan1958nonparametric}): it captures the ``stability'' of correctness under repeated questioning rather than a single-turn accuracy snapshot (``accuracy at round $r$ only'').

\textbf{Survival vs. per-round accuracy.}
For contrast, define the (marginal) per-round accuracy at round $r$ as
\(\mathrm{Acc}^{(t)}@r = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim S}[c^{(t)}_{r}(x)]\).
Unlike survival, \(\mathrm{Acc}@r\) counts an example as correct at round $r$ even if it was incorrect in an earlier round; thus \(\mathrm{Acc}^{(t)}@r\ge \mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@r\) always holds.
For example, if an item is correct at $r{=}1$, incorrect at $r{=}2$, and correct again at $r{=}3$, then \(\mathrm{Acc}@3\) counts it as correct while \(\mathrm{Survival}@3\) does not (since it already deviated at $r{=}2$).
We report survival because it matches the operational question ``did the model ever abandon the gold answer up to this point?''.

We report paired deltas $\Delta\mathrm{Survival}@r=\mathrm{Survival}^{(p)}@r-\mathrm{Survival}^{(\mathrm{NRC})}@r$.

\paragraph{Discrete hazard (per-round failure risk).}
Survival curves can be re-expressed as a per-round failure ``hazard'' (conditional risk). Define $\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@0\equiv 1$ and, for $r\ge 1$,
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Hazard}^{(t)}@r
&\;=\; \Pr\Big[c^{(t)}_{r}(x)=0\,\Big|\,\prod_{i=1}^{r-1} c^{(t)}_{i}(x)=1\Big] \\
&\;=\; 1-\frac{\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@r}{\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@{(r-1)}}.
\end{align}
With this convention, $\mathrm{Hazard}^{(t)}@1 = 1-\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@1$ is exactly \FailAt{1}.
If $\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@{(r-1)}=0$ (no remaining at-risk examples), then the conditional hazard at round $r$ is undefined; in practice our reported plots/tables omit hazards beyond the point where the survival curve reaches zero.
The hazard view makes clear whether a persona concentrates failures immediately (high \(\mathrm{Hazard}@1\)---equivalently, high \FailAt{1}) or gradually increases failure risk over rounds (rising hazards).

\paragraph{Time-to-first-failure (\TOF) and \FailAt{1}.}
Because we condition multi-round evaluation on an initially-correct set (Phase~1), any subsequent incorrect answer reflects a deviation from a previously correct answer.
Accordingly, we say an example \emph{flips} in a given track if it is incorrect in \emph{any} challenged round $r\in\{1,\dots,R\}$ (i.e., if $c^{(t)}_{r}(x)=0$ for some $r$).
Define the (possibly censored) first-failure time over the \emph{challenged} rounds $r\in\{1,\dots,R\}$ (the final recovery turn is \emph{not} included):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{TOF}^{(t)}(x)
\;=\; \min\big\{\, r\;:\; c^{(t)}_{r}(x)=0 \,\big\}.
\end{equation}
Here, the minimum ranges over challenged rounds $r\in\{1,\dots,R\}$. If no failure occurs in rounds $1\ldots R$, we set $\mathrm{TOF}^{(t)}(x)=R{+}1$ (right-censoring). 
We summarize \TOF at the population level using the expectation $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim S}[\mathrm{TOF}^{(t)}(x)]$ (with right-censoring represented as $R{+}1$). This expectation has a simple identity in terms of the survival curve (recall $\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@0\equiv 1$):
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim S}[\mathrm{TOF}^{(t)}(x)] \;=\; \sum_{r=0}^{R} \mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@r.
\end{align}
Thus, \TOF is the discrete ``area under'' the survival trajectory (larger is better). This follows from the tail-sum identity
\(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{TOF}]=\sum_{r=0}^{R}\Pr[\mathrm{TOF}>r]\) together with \(\Pr[\mathrm{TOF}>r]=\mathrm{Survival}@r\) under our definitions.
Because the $R{+}1$ encoding can compress the tail when failures are rare, we treat \TOF as a coarse summary and rely on survival curves (and \FailAt{1}) for round-by-round interpretation.
We report the drift-corrected gap
\begin{align}
\Delta\mathrm{TOF}
&\;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim S}[\mathrm{TOF}^{(p)}(x)] \\
&\quad\;\;\; -\mathbb{E}_{x\sim S}[\mathrm{TOF}^{(\mathrm{NRC})}(x)].
\end{align}
We define the first-turn failure rate as $\mathrm{Fail}^{(t)}@1\equiv 1-\mathrm{Survival}^{(t)}@1$ (equivalently, $\mathrm{Fail}^{(t)}@1=\mathrm{Hazard}^{(t)}@1$). We refer to this metric as \FailAt{1} and analogously report $\Delta\mathrm{Fail}@1$. 

\paragraph{\RecoveryAtFlip.}
For each track $t\in\{p,\mathrm{NRC}\}$, let $F^{(t)}=\{x\in S: \exists r\le R\;\text{s.t. }c^{(t)}_{r}(x)=0\}$ denote examples that \emph{fail at least once} (i.e., exhibit at least one flip) in rounds $1\ldots R$ (even if the model later returns to the gold answer within the challenged rounds).
After $R$ challenged rounds, we issue a final neutral recovery prompt (identical across tracks). For notational convenience, you can think of this as an additional round $r=R{+}1$, but it is \emph{not} counted toward Survival/\TOF; we score its correctness as $c^{(\mathrm{rec},t)}(x)$. This isolates ``can a neutral reset recover truth after any deviation'' from the within-round dynamics captured by Survival/\TOF.
We report the conditional recovery rate
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Recovery}^{(t)}@\mathrm{flip}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim F^{(t)}}\big[c^{(\mathrm{rec},t)}(x)\big],
\end{align}
and the paired difference $\Delta\mathrm{Recovery}@\mathrm{flip}=\mathrm{Recovery}^{(p)}@\mathrm{flip}-\mathrm{Recovery}^{(\mathrm{NRC})}@\mathrm{flip}$.
\textbf{Interpretation note (track-dependent denominators).} Because flips are defined \emph{within each track}, the recovery denominator can differ: $F^{(p)}$ (flipped under pressure) need not equal $F^{(\mathrm{NRC})}$ (flipped under control). Accordingly, $\Delta\,\RecoveryAtFlip$ is \emph{not} a paired per-example comparison; it answers a within-track question: \emph{``conditional on having flipped at least once in this track, does a final neutral reset recover the gold answer?''} This also means both signs can be denominator-driven: pressure may introduce additional (possibly harder) flips, lowering recovery, while a positive gap can arise if the flipped subset under pressure is systematically easier to recover on. A strictly paired alternative would require issuing the recovery prompt to both tracks for a shared flipped set (e.g., $F^{(p)}\cup F^{(\mathrm{NRC})}$), which we leave as an extension.
\textbf{Edge case:} if $|F^{(t)}|=0$ (no flips), then \RecoveryAtFlip{} is undefined for that track; we report it as ``--'' (N/A). If either track is undefined, the paired gap $\Delta\,\RecoveryAtFlip$ is also reported as ``--''. We exclude undefined values from any task-pooled averages. \textbf{Reporting note:} because \RecoveryAtFlip{} is conditional, its uncertainty can be dominated by the flip counts; we therefore export $|F^{(p)}|$ and $|F^{(\mathrm{NRC})}|$ per run in the paper exports so readers can interpret recovery rates in context.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We summarize multi-turn robustness as complementary views: (i) \textbf{\SurvivalAt{r}} captures cumulative correctness under repeated questioning, (ii) \textbf{\TOF and \FailAt{1}} capture early-turn brittleness and the onset of failure, and (iii) \textbf{\RecoveryAtFlip} captures whether a model can return to the gold answer after it has deviated.
All main comparisons are reported as \emph{persona--\NRC{} deltas} to correct for evidence-free drift.

\paragraph{One-stop summary.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} provides a compact, cross-family summary: across model families, pressure typically reduces \SurvivalAt{5} (negative $\Delta\,\SurvivalAt{5}$) and increases early-turn failures (positive $\Delta\,\FailAt{1}$) beyond evidence-free drift under \NRC{}.
\RecoveryAtFlip{} can be less tightly coupled: some settings show substantial robustness loss but relatively small recovery gaps, reinforcing that brittleness and recoverability are distinct axes (Appx.~\ref{sec:appendix-extra-results}).
Figures in this section decompose Table~\ref{tab:main} into dynamics (Fig.~\ref{fig:survival}), early hazard (Fig.~\ref{fig:fail1}), and cross-family replication (Fig.~\ref{fig:crossfam}).

\paragraph{Persona aggregate (equal-weight over personas; for compact reporting).}
Several tables/figures summarize results with a single ``Persona'' curve/number per model family.
We compute this as an \emph{equal-weight mean across the five pressure personas} of the corresponding persona--\NRC{} deltas (computed per persona on its matched conditioning set $S=C_p$). \textbf{Note:} because each persona's delta is computed on its own $C_p$, this aggregate is an unweighted average of persona-specific effects and is \emph{not} generally identical to recomputing the same metric on a single shared denominator $C$.
Concretely, for a metric $m$ and persona set $\mathcal{P}$,
\begin{align}
\Delta \bar{m} \;=\; \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|} \sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}} \Big(m^{(p)} - m^{(\mathrm{NRC},p)}\Big),
\end{align}
where $m^{(\mathrm{NRC},p)}$ denotes the NRC track paired to persona $p$ under matched decoding.
\textbf{Table interpretation note.} In Table~\ref{tab:main}, the \emph{Persona} and \emph{NRC} columns are computed as equal-weight averages of the underlying (non-delta) metric values across personas and their paired controls, respectively:
\(
\bar{m}^{(\mathrm{Persona})}=\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}} m^{(p)},\;
\bar{m}^{(\mathrm{NRC})}=\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}} m^{(\mathrm{NRC},p)}
\).
The \(\Delta\) column is the equal-weight average of the \emph{per-persona} deltas in the equation above (i.e., we average within each persona--\NRC{} pairing before aggregating across personas), so in general \(\Delta\bar{m}\neq \bar{m}^{(\mathrm{Persona})}-\bar{m}^{(\mathrm{NRC})}\). This keeps the drift correction aligned to each persona's matched control.

\paragraph{Task aggregate and seed aggregation (used for headline numbers).}
When we report a single headline number per model family (including the main aggregated setting referenced in the abstract), we aggregate persona--\NRC{} \emph{deltas} in three stages: (i) compute deltas within each persona--NRC pairing on its matched conditioning set $S=C_p$, (ii) take an equal-weight mean over the five personas, and (iii) take an equal-weight mean over the five tasks.
When multiple random seeds are available, we compute this task\,$\times$\,persona aggregated delta separately per seed, then report mean\,\(\pm\)\,std over seeds.
This ordering (delta-first, then averaging) keeps drift correction aligned to each persona's paired control before pooling across tasks/personas.

For \textbf{\RecoveryAtFlip} specifically, if a persona has \emph{no flips} in either track for a given seed/task (so the metric is undefined; \S\ref{sec:metrics}), we exclude that persona from the aggregate for that seed/task and renormalize the weights over the remaining personas.
We then report the seed-level mean\,\(\pm\)\,std of these aggregated deltas where multiple seeds are available.
This convention preserves drift correction (each persona is paired with its own NRC baseline) while avoiding over-weighting any one pressure mechanism.

\subsection{Survival degrades under pressure beyond generic drift}
Figure~\ref{fig:survival} shows survival curves over rounds for representative personas alongside NRC.
Table~\ref{tab:main} summarizes this effect at the horizon: for every model family we test, the persona aggregate shows \emph{negative} $\Delta\,\SurvivalAt{5}$ (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B: $\Delta\,\SurvivalAt{5}\approx -0.23$; Qwen2.5-14B: $\Delta\,\SurvivalAt{5}\approx -0.25$; Mistral-7B: $\Delta\,\SurvivalAt{5}\approx -0.31$), indicating substantial pressure-induced deviations beyond evidence-free drift.

Two patterns recur across tasks and seeds.
First, the persona--\NRC{} gap often \emph{widens with rounds}: even when Survival@1 is similar, repeated pressure can accumulate into a substantial \SurvivalAt{5} deficit.
Second, different personas exhibit distinct hazard shapes: some concentrate failures immediately (high \FailAt{1}, i.e., high \(\mathrm{Hazard}@1\)), while others gradually increase failure risk over turns.
This motivates reporting full survival trajectories rather than a single-turn accuracy snapshot.

\paragraph{How to read survival curves.}
Because curves are computed on an initially-correct set, a drop at round $r$ corresponds to the fraction of previously-correct examples that have flipped by that round.
Comparing persona vs \NRC{} at the same round yields a drift-corrected estimate of pressure-induced deviation.

\subsection{Time-to-first-failure highlights early vulnerability}
Figure~\ref{fig:fail1} reports persona-wise \(\Delta\)\FailAt{1}.
Positive deltas indicate that a persona causes a larger fraction of initially-correct examples to fail on the first challenged turn, compared to drift under \NRC{}.
At the aggregate level, Table~\ref{tab:main} shows that the persona-aggregated $\Delta\,\FailAt{1}$ is typically positive (e.g., Mistral-7B and Phi-3-mini show $\Delta\,\FailAt{1}\approx 0.24\text{--}0.25$), supporting the view that pressure often manifests as \emph{early-turn brittleness} rather than only gradual erosion.
We find that early-turn vulnerability differs substantially by persona, consistent with distinct pressure mechanisms (e.g., deference to authority vs logical contradiction).

\paragraph{Why \FailAt{1} is complementary to \SurvivalAt{5}.}
\SurvivalAt{5} aggregates failures over the full horizon, but can hide whether errors occur immediately or only after sustained interaction.
\FailAt{1} isolates the first challenged turn and is exactly the discrete hazard at $r=1$ (\S\ref{sec:metrics}), making it a sensitive indicator of brittle compliance.

\subsection{Recovery is a distinct axis from robustness}
We find that ``staying correct'' and ``returning to truth after a deviation'' are separable model behaviors: personas that substantially reduce \SurvivalAt{5} do not necessarily reduce \RecoveryAtFlip{}.
This matters because a deployment can be brittle (high \FailAt{1}) yet still recover under a neutral reset, suggesting that robustness and recovery-oriented guardrails target different failure modes.

\paragraph{Why \RecoveryAtFlip is conditional.}
Recovery is computed only on examples that flipped at least once in the corresponding track.
This prevents denominators from being dominated by easy examples that never deviate, and makes the metric directly interpretable as ``given a deviation occurred, how often can the model return to truth?'' (\S\ref{sec:metrics}).

For space, we report the full persona-wise \(\Delta\)\RecoveryAtFlip plots and the decoding sweep in the Appendix (Appx.~\ref{sec:appendix-extra-results}).

\subsection{Cross-family generalization}
Figure~\ref{fig:crossfam} tests whether the observed persona--\NRC{} gaps are specific to a single model family.
Across multiple open-weight families, we observe the same qualitative trend: a strong evidence-free pressure persona (Logical Trap) reduces \SurvivalAt{5} relative to NRC.
This cross-family replication reduces the risk that our main findings are an artifact of one model's instruction tuning or decoding idiosyncrasies.

\paragraph{Aggregate view across families.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} shows that the pressure-induced robustness gap is not confined to one model lineage: across the five families reported, the persona aggregate has negative $\Delta\,\SurvivalAt{5}$, with effects ranging from moderate (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B: $\approx$\,-0.07) to large (e.g., Mistral-7B: $\approx$\,-0.31).
Moreover, several families exhibit positive $\Delta\,\FailAt{1}$ (e.g., Phi-3-mini and Mistral-7B: $\approx$\,0.24--0.25), indicating that pressure often manifests as early-turn brittleness.
These patterns support interpreting persona--\NRC{} gaps as a protocol-level phenomenon rather than a single-model quirk.

\paragraph{What cross-family does (and does not) claim.}
Cross-family replication supports the \emph{existence} and qualitative direction of the effect (pressure increases deviation beyond drift), but it does not imply a single universal ranking of personas or tasks.
Differences in instruction tuning and refusal behavior can shift which pressure mechanisms dominate \FailAt{1} versus gradual degradation, so we emphasize the delta-based protocol and provide persona-wise decompositions in the Appendix.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Findings and implications.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Pressure effects are measurable beyond generic multi-turn drift.} Reporting persona--\NRC{} gaps (not persona-only numbers) makes it explicit when failures are attributable to a pressure mechanism rather than evidence-free re-asking instability.
  \item \textbf{Robustness is a trajectory, not a scalar.} Survival curves reveal whether pressure accumulates gradually or concentrates immediately; \FailAt{1} isolates the first challenged turn as a sensitive brittleness indicator.
  \item \textbf{Brittleness and recoverability can decouple.} Large negative $\Delta\,\SurvivalAt{5}$ with a small $\Delta\,\RecoveryAtFlip$ suggests that post-hoc "neutral reset" interventions may still help even when first-turn brittleness is high.
  \item \textbf{Cross-family replication matters for claims.} Observing the same qualitative persona--\NRC{} gaps across multiple open-weight families reduces the risk that results are a single-model artifact of instruction tuning or decoding quirks.
  \item \textbf{Conditioning clarifies what is being measured.} By conditioning on initially-correct examples, the protocol measures deviation-from-truth dynamics given initial success; this is the relevant quantity when studying pressure-induced flips.
  \item \textbf{Practical evaluation guidance.} For deployed assistants, we recommend reporting (i) Survival@1\ldots R, (ii) \FailAt{1}, (iii) \RecoveryAtFlip{}, and always pairing pressure prompts with an evidence-free matched control like NRC.
  \item \textbf{Deployment guardrails suggested by the metrics.} High \FailAt{1} motivates front-end mitigations (e.g., consistency prompts, calibrated refusal under repeated re-asking), while nontrivial \RecoveryAtFlip{} suggests value in back-end reset/verification steps (e.g., verify-then-answer, neutral re-solve). Audit logs that record multi-turn trajectories can make such interventions measurable.
  \item \textbf{Reporting should include denominators.} Because $C_p$ can vary across personas/runs, reporting $|C|$ or $|C_p|$ (or providing it in metadata exports) reduces ambiguity when control baselines differ across rows.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Recommended reporting bundle.}
For papers and internal evaluations, we suggest treating the following as a minimal reporting bundle: (i) a protocol figure (Phase~1--3), (ii) a main table with cross-family aggregates (Table~\ref{tab:main}), (iii) at least one dynamics figure (survival curves), and (iv) an Appendix with prompt templates and per-persona decompositions.
Crucially, each reported number should be backed by an auditable export bundle (metadata JSON + per-example CSV), so that readers can re-derive the table/figure without relying on undocumented evaluator heuristics.

\paragraph{Open questions.}
We view \GALILEO{} as a starting point for auditing multi-turn reliability. Several extensions are important:
(i) longer-horizon dialogues and long-context effects,
(ii) evidence-grounded counterarguments and belief revision,
(iii) tool-augmented settings where actions can compound mistakes,
and (iv) intervention design (e.g., verification, consistency checks) that improves both robustness and recoverability.

\paragraph{Implications for benchmark design.}
Our findings suggest that benchmark design choices can materially affect conclusions about multi-turn robustness.
First, without an evidence-free matched control, it is easy to over-attribute generic re-asking instability to a specific pressure mechanism.
Second, reporting only end-point scalars (e.g., \SurvivalAt{5} alone) can hide whether a failure mode is dominated by immediate compliance (\FailAt{1}) or by cumulative degradation across rounds.
Third, recovery should be treated as a conditional capability: a model may be brittle under pressure but still recover when given a neutral reset, which motivates evaluating both axes explicitly.

Finally, we recommend that evaluation suites publish machine-readable exports (per-example trajectories + metadata) as first-class artifacts.
This reduces ambiguity in answer extraction and normalization, and it allows independent re-aggregation (e.g., different persona weightings) without rerunning expensive inference.
In our setup, the same export bundle supports both paper-facing figures and additional appendix decompositions.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\pagemark{LIMITATIONS_SECTION_START}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Protocol scope.} \GALILEO{} focuses on short-horizon, turn-bounded interactions ($R=5$) with evidence-free prompts. Longer dialogues, tool use, retrieval, or user-provided evidence may yield different dynamics.
  \item \textbf{What \NRC{} does (and does not) represent.} \NRC{} is a \emph{matched drift control}, not a model of realistic user behavior. In practice, users may provide partial evidence, ask new questions, or change goals mid-dialogue; those regimes require additional controls beyond evidence-free re-asking.
  \item \textbf{Conditioning-set bias.} Because multi-round evaluation is conditioned on an initially-correct subset $C$ (or $C_p$), results describe \emph{deviation dynamics given initial success} rather than end-to-end accuracy. Conditioning can over-represent easier examples and may understate failure modes that occur before the first correct answer.
  \item \textbf{Recovery denominator and conditional prompting.} \RecoveryAtFlip{} is defined \emph{conditional on having flipped}. Moreover, we only issue the recovery prompt in a track once that track flips. Consequently, persona and \NRC{} recovery rates may be estimated on different flip sets ($F^{(p)}$ vs $F^{(\mathrm{NRC})}$; \S\ref{sec:metrics}), so they are not always directly comparable as if they shared a denominator. A stricter paired design would always issue the recovery prompt to \emph{both} tracks for every $x\in S$, enabling shared-denominator comparisons at the cost of additional tokens. We therefore recommend always reporting flip denominators (e.g., $|F^{(p)}|$ and $|F^{(\mathrm{NRC})}|$) and uncertainty (e.g., bootstrap CIs) alongside \RecoveryAtFlip{}.
  \item \textbf{Task coverage.} We emphasize ground-truth tasks with unambiguous scoring. This excludes many open-ended domains (e.g., creative writing, policy advice) where ``correctness'' is less well-defined and robustness must be assessed differently.
  \item \textbf{Answer extraction and scoring artifacts.} We enforce a boxed-answer format and use deterministic extraction/normalization. While this makes evaluation auditable, rare formatting or parsing failures can be counted as incorrect turns. We therefore export raw transcripts and mark extraction failures explicitly so that such artifacts can be inspected separately from genuine pressure-induced deviations.
  \item \textbf{Persona design.} Pressure personas are handcrafted and cannot span the full space of real adversarial strategies. \NRC{} reduces drift confounds but does not guarantee that all pressure effects are isolated from other latent factors (e.g., wording sensitivity).
  \item \textbf{Model scope.} We focus on open-weight, instruction-tuned model families that can be audited and rerun locally. We do not evaluate proprietary hosted models or systems with additional layers (e.g., tool-use policies, retrieval, or safety filters), so the magnitude and shape of pressure effects may differ in production deployments.
  \item \textbf{Model/decode dependence.} Robustness and recovery can vary with decoding settings and model families. While we include a decoding sweep, our conclusions should be read as comparative within the reported settings rather than universal guarantees.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ethics Statement}
\label{sec:ethics}
\pagemark{ETHICS_SECTION_START}
We study evidence-free conversational pressure that can cause a model to abandon a previously correct answer.
While this improves our understanding of multi-turn reliability, it also carries misuse risk: similar prompts could be used to intentionally destabilize deployed systems.
To mitigate this risk, we:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item report aggregate trends and drift-corrected gaps (persona -- \NRC{}) rather than curating or optimizing ``best attack'' prompts against any specific deployed system;
  \item restrict our evaluation to open-weight models that can be run and audited locally, and we do not test against third-party deployed assistants without explicit authorization;
  \item release prompt templates and per-example exports for reproducibility, but avoid packaging them as a turnkey ``destabilization kit'' (e.g., no model-specific prompt tuning aimed at maximizing flips);
  \item recommend pairing any pressure evaluation with an evidence-free matched control (\NRC{}) and adding deployment guardrails (e.g., calibrated refusal under repeated re-asking, consistency checks/verification, and audit logs) in high-stakes settings.
\end{itemize}

To support reproducibility and auditing, we plan to release the evaluation code, prompt templates, and per-example export bundles for the reported runs (subject to the underlying dataset licenses and any model distribution terms) upon publication.

Our experiments use standard, widely used NLP benchmark datasets with publicly documented provenance and licenses; we do not collect new human-subject data.
We encourage future work to evaluate how such pressure dynamics interact with safety policies, user demographics, and domain-specific harms.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced \textbf{\GALILEO{}}, a reproducible evaluation protocol for \emph{multi-turn} robustness on ground-truth tasks that reports survival trajectories, time-to-first-failure (\TOF) and first-turn failure (\FailAt{1}), and recovery after deviation (\RecoveryAtFlip{}).
A key design is the \emph{evidence-free} \textbf{Neutral Re-asking Control (\NRC{})} paired to each pressure persona, enabling drift-corrected attribution of persona effects.
Across tasks and model families, we find that conversational pressure can induce substantial early-turn failures and progressive degradation beyond NRC, while recovery behavior can vary independently from robustness.
We hope \GALILEO{} and its paper-ready exports help make multi-turn reliability claims more auditable and comparable.
